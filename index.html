Beyond the Cheon‑Kozaki​‑Kutsuma​‑Matsuo attack

The problemWhat the challenge hands you

BLS12-381 is an elliptic curve defined over a 381-bit prime field Fp (so point coordinates (x, y) are integers modulo p). Inside it sits the subgroup G1 of prime order r ≈ 2254.86, generated by a base point P0. A secret scalar s is chosen from the scalar field Fr (integers modulo r), and you are given the generator P0, the target point T = [s]P0, and the auxiliary powers

Ai = [si]P0   for   i = 1 … n,   n = 220

This is the shape of a powers-of-tau trusted setup. Recover s.

In the curvesma.sh challenge, a valid submission must recover s with at least a 39% success probability (chosen to match 1 − e−1/2 ≈ 0.393). Total cost is measured in normalized reference group operations, where one unit represents a single batched affine point addition over Fp (six base-field multiplications, fixed at C = 2,000 instructions).

Without the auxiliary powers, plain Pollard rho using the curve's six-element automorphism classes costs about 2126.5 operations. The auxiliary points Ai are what make a faster attack possible — and the history below is about how much they are worth.

2006Cheon's reduction

Cheon's reduction requires a positive divisor d of r − 1 (or of r + 1 in a Dickson-polynomial variant we will not need here) no larger than the supply of powers n. BLS12-381 is generous here, and not by accident: r was chosen so that 232 divides r − 1, which allows radix-2 FFTs over Fr, which make SNARK proving faster. This property is what the attack feeds on — every divisor d = 2k up to k = 32 is available, and the challenge supplies powers up to n = 220, so we take d = 220.

Write s = ζk for a generator ζ of Fr*, the multiplicative group of non-zero scalars, which is cyclic of order r − 1. Then sd lies in H, the subgroup of d-th powers in Fr*, which has order

m = (r − 1) / d ≈ 2234.86

— a search space 220 times smaller than the one s lives in. Phase 1 finds k mod m by a baby-step giant-step (BSGS) meet-in-the-middle inside H using two lists of size L = ⌈√m⌉ ≈ 2117.43; Phase 2 then recovers the remaining 20 bits of k by a second, tiny search over the d-th roots of unity using two lists of size √d = 210.

Because d = 220, Cheon's reduction cuts the number of steps by a factor of √d = 210 (ten full bits), down to 2√(r/d) ≈ 2118.43 steps in Phase 1 and 2√d = 211 steps in Phase 2. In Cheon's 2006 analysis, however, each step required a full scalar multiplication — O(log r) group operations — so the published complexity carried a logarithmic factor that ate back most of those ten bits until 2007:

O( (√(r/d) + √d) · log r )

So the divisor is frozen at d = 220, and has been since 2006. Everything that follows is a fight over what one step costs — first the log r factor, then the constant.

2007KKM: kill the log factor

Why did Cheon's 2006 paper only gain 1.3 bits over Pollard rho when √d = √(220) = 210 cuts the step count by a full 10 bits? Because in Cheon's 2006 analysis, advancing from [ζdi]P0 to [ζd(i+1)]P0 meant multiplying the point by the 255-bit scalar ζd — a 383-operation binary scalar multiplication (+8.6 bits per step) that ate back almost the entire 10-bit step reduction.

Kozaki, Kutsuma, and Matsuo (KKM) observed that every point in the walk is a multiple of the same fixed base point (Ad for the baby steps, P0 for the giant steps). By expanding each scalar δi = ζdi mod r in base b = ⌈r1/c⌉ and precomputing a table of c · b points, any [δi]P0 can be assembled by adding c − 1 table lookups. With c = 3 tables, a step drops to:

  • one Fr multiplication — update the scalar exponent, δi+1 = δi · ζd mod r
  • two point additions — sum the c = 3 base-b digits from the precomputed tables

That reduces the per-step cost from O(log r) to O(1) group operations, flattening the asymptotic complexity to O(√(r/d) + √d) and unlocking the remaining 7.3 bits of Cheon's reduction. This is the only asymptotic improvement in the entire lineage — everything after it is a fight over the constant factor.

Deriving the 2117.894 KKM baseline (39% birthday bound + 6-fold GLV folding + KKM step cost). How do we get the exact 2117.894 cost for KKM in the benchmark? Start from textbook BSGS's 2L = 2√m ≈ 2118.43 steps and apply the two standard reductions plus KKM's per-step cost:

1. The 39% birthday target (−0.47 bits in steps): Testing K · m random pairs in a subgroup of size m finds a collision with probability 1 − e−K. Targeting the benchmark's ≥ 39% requirement uses K = 0.52 (1 − e−0.52 ≈ 40.5%) instead of K = 1, shrinking each list by √0.52 ≈ 2−0.47.
2. Six-fold GLV folding for free (−1.29 bits in steps): BLS12-381's G1 is a j = 0 curve carrying the cube-root endomorphism λ: (x, y) ↦ (βx, y), which together with negation (x, y) ↦ (x, p − y) forms six-element classes inside H. Because λ fixes the y-coordinate and negation only flips its sign modulo p, keying the lookup table on min(y, p − y) makes all six points [±1, ±λ, ±λ2]Q collide on the same key with zero extra multiplications. Testing six ratios per pair shrinks each list by √6 ≈ 21.29.
3. KKM's cost per step (+1.23 bits per step): Those two reductions bring the number of evaluated steps down to 2N = 2√(Km/6) ≈ 2116.66. In KKM (c = 3), however, each step costs 2 curve additions (2.0 units) + 1 Fr multiplication (≈ 0.11 units) + table/routing overhead (0.245 units) = 2.353 ≈ 21.234 reference units per step.

Multiplying the reduced step count by KKM's per-step cost gives KKM's total benchmark score: 2116.66 steps × 2.353 units/step = 2117.894 reference units.

October 2026The additive walk

The first curvesma.sh submission to beat the 117.894 KKM baseline kept Cheon's subgroup reduction untouched and replaced the walk itself. It abandoned the traditional geometric progression (xi+1 = xi · ζd) in H and replaced it with lists of integers that can be walked using a single elliptic curve addition.

A nineteen-year-old road not taken. Walking through exponents by adding small integer steps from a precomputed jump table goes back to Pollard's 1978 "kangaroo" (or λ) method, though it had never been successfully combined with Cheon's reduction. Remarkably, Kozaki, Kutsuma, and Matsuo (§4 of their 2007 paper) explicitly considered walking small integers {gt, [2]gt, …, [n−1]gt} in the context of Cheon's attack, but abandoned the idea, remarking: "However, a table in this scenario seems difficult to construct because the elements in {gt, [2]gt, [3]gt, …, [n−1]gt} are discretely distributed in Z/pZ in general."

Why a step was always expensive

In both textbook Cheon and KKM, the baby and giant sets are geometric progressions in H: ζ0, ζd, ζ2d, …. Advancing one step multiplies the scalar exponent by the 255-bit constant ζd mod r, landing at a pseudorandom position across Fr. Because consecutive scalars have no simple additive difference, you cannot step directly from one curve point to the next: Cheon rebuilds the point from scratch with a 383-operation scalar multiplication, and KKM still has to multiply the scalar in Fr and sum two table additions to reassemble it (2.353 units per step).

Where KKM left the geometric sets alone and sped up evaluating them, the additive walk changes the sets so that consecutive scalars in H are integers of the form a · b differing by a small known gap a · g. Advancing from one point to the next becomes

[a(b + g)]P0 = [ab]P0 + T[g]

— a single affine curve addition using a small gap table T[g] = [ag]P0, with no exponent multiplication in Fr at all. Dropping from KKM's two additions and an Fr multiply (2.353 units) to a single addition (1.245 units) immediately cuts 0.91 bits off the total score (117.894 → 116.983).

Finding enough integers in H

Why did KKM think such a walk was difficult to construct? Because a random integer lands in the subgroup H with probability only 1/d = 2−20, making naive enumerate-and-test hopeless. The fix is a multiplicative character sieve: define χ(x) = logζ(xm) mod d, a homomorphism whose kernel is H. A product a · b lies in H precisely when

χ(a) + χ(b) ≡ 0  (mod d)

We can compute χ(b) for all small integers b once and bucket them into lists Lc = {b : χ(b) = c}. Then, for any base prime a with character χ(a) = −c, every integer b ∈ Lc satisfies a · b ∈ H, and walking through sorted elements of Lc steps by small integer gaps g = bi+1 − bi.

Making one addition cost one reference unit

An affine curve addition over Fp requires one field inversion (to compute the chord slope) and three field multiplications (3M). A standalone inversion costs dozens of field multiplications, and a single walk is strictly sequential — you need Xi before you can compute Xi+1. To amortize the inversion, 216 chains run in lockstep, one per base prime a, and all 216 independent additions in a round share a single Montgomery batch inversion (which costs 3M per point, bringing the step to the 6M reference unit).

Remark: The H-prime semiprime variation (jungjipdo). A follow-up submission simplified the character construction by setting both characters to zero: χ(a) = χ(b) = 0, with a and b both chosen to be primes lying directly in H ("H-primes"). Because every scalar x = a · b is then a semiprime, unique prime factorization immediately guarantees that all walk elements and nearly all ratios are distinct without any overlap analysis. Algorithmically, however, restricting b to primes in H thins the candidate pool by a factor of ln b, making valid steps rarer and gaps much wider. Although the submission achieved a better leaderboard score at the time, that gain came purely from tighter bookkeeping and implementation accounting rather than the semiprime walk itself.

The accounting eraRouting and coverage trims

Once the additive walk was in place, four days of submissions — by Yonkoo11, kaankolcu, and fkiene — fought over the non-cryptographic overhead charged on top of the curve addition:

  • Table routing overhead dropped from 0.245 to 0.070 units per element. In the benchmark's distributed memory model, matching N ≈ 2115.66 baby steps against N giant steps across 284 memory nodes was initially dominated by multi-hop packet routing. Replacing per-element routing with direct-mapped bucket sorting cut the transport cost by more than two-thirds.
  • The coverage constant K was trimmed from 0.52 down to 0.49515. Setting K = 0.52 gave a collision probability of 1 − e−0.52 ≈ 40.55%, overshooting the benchmark's required 39% threshold; tuning K down to the exact requirement saved another 0.035 bits for free.

27–30 OctoberTwo points, one inversion

Everything above treated one batched affine addition (6M) as the irreducible floor for generating one search element. That turned out to be a convention, not a floor.

Found twice, hours apart. gbotrel filed the construction first at 01:08; mpjunior92 reached it independently at 07:29 and was first to get it accepted at 09:41. The winning packages now cite each other.

Suppose a character class Lc contains two elements b1 = b2 − g and b3 = b2 + g symmetric around a midpoint b2. If the worker holds the midpoint curve point P = [a · b2]Q (for Q ∈ {P0, Ad}) and looks up the gap point T = [a · g]Q, then both outer points are one addition away:

[a(b2 + g)]Q = P + T     [a(b2 − g)]Q = P − T

Crucially, on an elliptic curve, −T = (xT, −yT) has the exact same x-coordinate as T = (xT, yT). Both chord slopes share the identical denominator Δx = xT − xP, so a single field inversion produces both points.

Why combining characters with 3-APs is not trivial

Merging this 2-for-1 inversion trick with the earlier subgroup walk runs into an immediate algebraic wall: the character sieve is multiplicative, whereas 3-term arithmetic progressions (3-APs) are additive.

For both P − T and P + T to represent valid search elements in H, both b1 = b2 − g and b3 = b2 + g must have the same multiplicative character χ(b1) = χ(b3) = −χ(a). Because χ is a homomorphism with respect to multiplication (χ(xy) = χ(x) + χ(y)), it has no predictable relationship with addition: knowing χ(b2) and χ(g) tells you nothing at all about χ(b2 ± g). Worse, trying to use the H-prime semiprime variant here fails outright: a midpoint works only when both b2 − g and b2 + g land in the list, so the density of usable pairs scales as the square of the list's density. Requiring 76-bit integers to be prime thins the list by ln(276) ≈ 53, making symmetric pairs about 532 ≈ 2,800 times rarer and blowing up the gap tables to 2110.

The solution decouples subgroup membership from walk scheduling:

  • Multiplicative sieving first: Small integers (or primes) b are sieved once by their character into dense buckets Lc = {b : χ(b) = c}.
  • Offline additive planning: Instead of walking Lc sequentially, an offline planning pass scans each sorted class Lc for naturally occurring symmetric pairs b1, b3 ∈ Lc (or 3-term progressions b1, b2, b3) with midpoint b2 and small gap g = b3 − b2 = b2 − b1.
  • Midpoint execution: The worker advances the chain from one midpoint P = [a · b2]Q to the next and uses the shared inversion with T = [a · g]Q to emit P − T and P + T simultaneously.

This achieves the best of both worlds — the high density of the character sieve together with the 2-for-1 inversion discount — at the price of two minor penalties that must be budgeted in the score. First, because elements in Lc are distributed pseudorandomly, they do not tile into pairs with 100% efficiency: roughly 98% of points are emitted in pairs, while the remaining ~2% of "orphaned" elements must be evaluated individually, slightly eroding the theoretical discount. Second, returning from H-primes to general character buckets (with prime a > b) reintroduces a tiny, bounded fraction of duplicate ratios across chains.

Recovering the last 20 bits

In textbook Cheon, Phase 1 finds the discrete logarithm k mod m directly. In the additive walk, a collision between a baby step [a · b]Ad and a giant step [a′ · b′]P0 instead yields the field element t = sd = ±λj(a′b′)/(ab) mod r as a ratio of known integers. Phase 2 therefore inverts the d-th power map in two brief steps:

  • Extract one d-th root in Fr: Because d = 220, taking 20 successive modular square roots of t via Tonelli–Shanks yields one root s0 ∈ Fr satisfying s0d ≡ t (mod r).
  • Identify which of the d roots is s: The true secret is s = s0 · ωi for some 0 ≤ i < 220, where ω = ζ(r−1)/d is a primitive d-th root of unity. Writing i = i0 + 210i1 and matching the 210 baby points [s0ωi0]P0 against the 210 giant points [ω−210i1]T recovers i in 2√d = 211 steps.

All of Phase 2 costs under 224 units — over ninety bits below Phase 1 and completely invisible in the total score.

The final fractions of a bit

Because the benchmark defines one reference unit as C = 2,000 executed AArch64 instructions, the final submissions (by mpjunior92, gbotrel, pepedesigner, kaankolcu, and e-man07) moved from high-level operation counts to benchmarking custom straight-line assembly for the paired addition and hoisting function-call overhead out of the inner loop across all 216 lanes. Cheon's underlying step count has not moved since 2006 and cannot move: it sits at the generic lower bound for discrete logarithms with auxiliary inputs. What the eight-day contest measured is how much room remained inside a single step — and the published state of the art turned out to be nearly 1.6 bits above the floor.
